# Can Machine Learning Help Predict Volcanic Eruptions?

This question has been central during the Ph.D. thesis of Matthieu Nougaret, defended last december. Matthieu looked at this problem from three different angles. Among those, we wondered if we could forecast the signals used to monitor volcanoes in order to improve eruption forecasting by volcano observatories. We just published a new paper presenting this research in *Comptes Rendus. Géosciences*: [**Geophysical time series forecasting at the Piton de la Fournaise volcano by machine learning**](https://comptes-rendus.academie-sciences.fr/geoscience/articles/10.5802/crgeos.335/), by Matthieu Nougaret, Charles Le Losq, Lise Retailleau, and Aline Peltier. 

This post gives an accessible overview of what we did and what we found.

---

## Why Monitor Volcanoes?

Volcanoes are awe-inspiring — and dangerous. Since the 14th century, volcanic activity has caused roughly 280,000 deaths worldwide. Knowing *when* a volcano is about to erupt can save lives. Volcano observatories around the world continuously monitor active volcanoes, looking for signs of unrest: subtle ground displacements, increased seismic activity, changes in gas emissions. The challenge is interpreting this flood of data in near real time.

## Piton de la Fournaise: A Natural Laboratory

Our study focuses on **Piton de la Fournaise**, a shield volcano on Réunion Island in the Indian Ocean. It is one of the most active volcanoes on Earth, erupting on average nearly twice a year over the last fifty years. This makes it an ideal place to develop and test new monitoring methods: there are plenty of eruptions to study, and the volcano has been continuously and thoroughly monitored since 1979 by the [Piton de la Fournaise Volcanological Observatory (OVPF-IPGP)](https://www.ipgp.fr/observation/ovs/ovpf/).

Among the different type of signals analysed by OVPF-IPGP, two are central to operational monitoring at this volcano:

- **Volcano-tectonic (VT) seismicity**: small earthquakes generated as magma forces its way through rock. A sudden spike in the daily count of these micro-quakes is one of the most reliable warning signs before an eruption.
- **GNSS deformation data**: GPS-style measurements of how far apart [GNSS](https://www.euspa.europa.eu/eu-space-programme/galileo/what-gnss) stations on the volcano's surface are from one another. In a volcano, as magma fills underground reservoirs, the ground deforms, modifying distances between GNSS stations. These subtle deformations — sometimes just a few millimetres per day — tell us about the pressurization of the magma plumbing system.

## Can Machine Learning Forecast These Signals?

Machine learning (ML) has transformed many fields by finding hidden patterns in large datasets. In volcanology, it has already been applied successfully to automatically detect and classify earthquakes. But can it go one step further and *forecast* what the monitoring signals will look like in the future? If we could reliably predict the next few days of seismicity or ground deformation, we could potentially anticipate dangerous accelerations before they trigger existing alarm thresholds — giving observatories more time to react.

That is the core question our paper tries to answer.

## What We Did

We trained and evaluated **six different forecasting models** on 16 years of data (2008–2023) from Piton de la Fournaise, covering 62 eruptions and magma intrusions. The models range from simple to complex:

1. **Last** — a naive baseline that simply repeats today's value as tomorrow's forecast.
2. **Linear regression** — a classical statistical model that learns a weighted combination of past observations.
3. **Dense neural network** — a standard "fully connected" artificial neural network.
4. **Convolutional neural network (CNN)** — originally designed for image analysis, CNNs are also effective at detecting local patterns in time series.
5. **LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)** — a type of recurrent neural network specifically designed to remember information over long sequences, well suited to time series.
6. **Transformer** — the architecture behind modern large language models (like ChatGPT), adapted here for time series forecasting. It uses a mechanism called *self-attention* to weigh the importance of each past time step when making a prediction.

We also tested a classical **GARCH model** from econometrics, which was designed for volatile, heteroskedastic time series (i.e., series whose variability changes over time — much like financial markets).

All models were asked to forecast the next **5 days** of signals, using several input features derived from the raw measurements: daily VT earthquake counts and GNSS baseline gradients computed over 5-, 10-, and 30-day windows.

## What We Found

### For seismicity, the Transformer wins — but the gains are modest

All ML models outperformed the naive baseline for forecasting seismicity. The **Transformer** came out on top, with slightly better scores than the other architectures, especially for longer forecast horizons (4–5 days ahead). However, all models, including the Transformer, share a common weakness: they react to abrupt changes rather than anticipate them. When seismicity starts ramping up steeply just before an eruption, the models systematically lag one day behind. They miss the sudden pre-eruptive acceleration — the very signal that matters most. Forecasts are reliable only for the next day or two under calm or decreasing seismic activity.

### For GNSS deformation, simpler is better

Here is a perhaps surprising result: for GNSS data, the **simple Linear model** performed best overall. More complex architectures like LSTM and Transformer actually scored *worse* than the linear baseline once we accounted for overall forecasting robustness. Why? The GNSS signals are dominated by long, slow trends punctuated by very rapid, short-lived accelerations during magma injection. Complex ML models, which need many training examples to leverage their capacity, seem to struggle with the rarity of eruption events and the highly irregular character of the short-term GNSS variations.

As with seismicity, none of the models managed to anticipate the sharp ground acceleration observed just before the July 2023 eruption. However, once the eruption started, forecasts of deformation during and after the event were much better.

### Combining seismicity and GNSS data doesn't help

One might expect that feeding both types of data together into a model would give it more information to work with. In practice, **combining the two datasets provided no clear improvement** over training separate models. The joint dataset is dominated by the many GNSS features, and the best model in the joint case was again the simple Linear model — very similar to what we found for GNSS alone.

### GARCH shows no advantage

The statistical [GARCH model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoregressive_conditional_heteroskedasticity), despite being designed for volatile time series, did not outperform the ML models for daily VT seismicity forecasting. Like all the other approaches, it failed to anticipate sudden pre-eruptive accelerations.

## Why Is This So Hard?

Our results point to several fundamental challenges:

- **Rare events, imbalanced data**: Piton de la Fournaise is at rest about 90% of the time. Pre-eruptive and eruptive signals are short, intense bursts in a sea of background noise. Complex models need many examples to learn from, but there are only ~62 eruptions in 16 years.
- **Missing information**: Daily earthquake counts and GNSS baseline gradients may simply not contain all the information needed to forecast their own evolution. Missing pieces include earthquake magnitudes and precise locations, the depth and volume of magma sources, and geochemical signals like CO₂ and SO₂.
- **Volcano-specific quirks**: The relationship between ground deformation and the monitoring signals varies depending on where exactly magma intrudes and the geometry of the monitoring network. Some GNSS station pairs are more informative than others.
- **Irregular precursor patterns**: The timing and amplitude of pre-eruptive signals vary enormously between eruptions at Piton de la Fournaise. This makes it very hard for any algorithm — or human expert — to learn a single, reliable template.

## What's Next?

Our results are a first step, and they open up several promising directions:

- **Richer data**: Including earthquake magnitudes, locations, source parameters from deformation modelling, and geochemical data (CO₂, SO₂) could provide models with the additional information they currently lack.
- **Synthetic data generation**: Physics-based simulations of magma transport, combined with data-driven generative models, could create synthetic pre-eruptive scenarios to augment the training dataset.
- **Transfer learning**: Pre-training models on monitoring data from many volcanoes around the world, then fine-tuning on Piton de la Fournaise, could help overcome the scarcity of training examples at any single site.
- **Probabilistic forecasting**: Rather than predicting a single "most likely" future, operational forecasting tools should quantify uncertainty — providing confidence intervals that inform risk assessments.
- **Hybrid physics-ML models**: Combining physical models of magma dynamics with data-driven ML could leverage the best of both worlds.

Even with their current limitations, ML forecasting models could complement the threshold-based alarm systems already in use at volcano observatories. Short-term forecasts of seismic or deformation trends — even imperfect ones — could provide early warnings of anomalous behaviour before critical thresholds are crossed.
